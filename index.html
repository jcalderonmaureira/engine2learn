Learning design, written so machines can read it

Objectives, activities, assessments and rules are specified explicitly instead of living in Word and Excel files. That explicit specification is the substrate for everything that follows.

Three questions about the same design

Can a design change while it runs? Can it be checked against quality standards? Can it respond to each learner? My work answers them one at a time, on one shared foundation.

Flexibility: designs that can change while they run

Courses are specified as static sequences; real classes are not. Swapping an activity at runtime can break a design: deadlocks, activities nobody can reach.

Flexibility: change, with formal guarantees

FlexLearn combines workflow, rules and pedagogical constraints, so a substitution keeps the design deadlock-free and every state reachable.

Compliance: designs that can be checked

Constructive alignment links objectives, activities, assessments and materials. In document-based designs those links are implicit, so verifying them is slow, manual work.

Compliance: explicit links, automatic check, human decision

Once the links are written down, a design can be checked against Quality Matters automatically. A person still decides what to change.

Compliance: AI inside a pipeline, not as an oracle

A design document is uploaded, structured into JSON-LD, augmented with glossary retrieval, analysed and formatted by separate reasoning steps, and reported back to the designer.

Adaptivity: one substrate, three levels

The same traces and the same explicit design serve the learner (adaptive feedback), the course (flexible design) and the program (quality assurance).

Adaptivity: GenAI can propose, verified rules decide

Rule-based feedback is predictable, auditable and aligned with the instructor’s voice. Formal methods are complemented, not replaced, by generative AI.

Adaptivity: the feedback loop

Signals from student work update a learner state; instructor-authored rules choose cognitive, metacognitive or motivational feedback; safeguards limit its rate and keep learners in control.

The study: a crossover design

Every student gets adaptive feedback for half of the deployment and standard feedback for the other half, at two universities. Each student is their own comparison, and carryover is tested rather than assumed away.

One lever within learning design

Adaptive feedback is not a stand-alone tutor. It works best inside a design that is flexible and checkable, which is why these three lines of work belong together.

Adaptive, real-time feedback in higher education

A cross-institutional study of adaptive feedback in introductory programming (CS1) at Universidad Andrés Bello, Chile, and the University of Alberta, Canada.

The aim: evaluate whether real-time, adaptive feedback improves self-regulation, learning and usability in introductory programming, across two institutional contexts.

  • Self-regulation RQ1

    Adaptive vs. traditional feedback: planning, monitoring and strategy use.

  • Who benefits RQ2

    Which learner characteristics predict effective tailoring.

  • What kind RQ3

    Cognitive, metacognitive or motivational feedback: which helps most.

  • How much RQ4

    Personalization without overwhelming learners.

Two sites, one design

Universidad Andrés Bello

CS1 for engineering programs. The adaptive course was delivered between March and August 2026.

University of Alberta

CS1-equivalent course. Instructor co-design and deployment are planned for the 2026–27 academic year, subject to ethics approval.

Companion strand: Quality Matters compliance of course designs at both sites.

Light-touch measurement

~190 → ~33self-report items

35–45 → 10–12minutes to complete

Self-regulation, motivation, self-efficacy, cognitive load, usability and learning, plus automatic behavioural traces.

Three kinds of feedback

  • Cognitive

    About the task: concept, error, strategy.

    “The loop never updates i. Trace the first two iterations by hand.”

  • Metacognitive

    About planning and monitoring.

    “Three runs, same error. What have you ruled out so far?”

  • Motivational

    About effort and confidence.

    “You traced a harder problem than last week. Real progress.”

Illustrative CS1 examples, not actual messages.

Designed for trust and fairness

  • Minimal risk, voluntary participation, no academic penalty for opting out.
  • Nobody loses standard feedback: the study varies how feedback is delivered, not whether.
  • Recruitment by the research team, not the instructor, who never sees who consented.
  • Safeguards in the tool: rate limiting, user controls, monitoring for overload.
  • Data pseudonymized and access-controlled, under UofA REB and Chilean Law 19.628.

What we hope to contribute

  1. Evidence on adaptive vs. standard feedback and self-regulation, in two contexts.
  2. A feature catalogue of learner signals that predict useful tailoring.
  3. A bridge between adaptive feedback and flexible, quality-assured design.
